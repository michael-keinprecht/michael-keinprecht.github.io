<h2 class="research-section">Working Papers</h2>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title"><a href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5962280">Third party loss aversion reduces spectator distribution</a></div>
  <p class="paper-status">Working paper</p>
  <details>
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p>In a preregistered online experiment with a within-subjects design, I show that redistribution decisions by third parties are affected by loss aversion (therefore third-party loss aversion). Overall, spectators are twice as likely (i.e., 7 percentage points more likely) to choose a status quo over an alternative if the alternative results in a loss for one person, even if the alternative is favored by inequality aversion, maximin preferences and efficiency concerns. The effect is larger when the loss is higher compared to the gain and when the spectator's individual loss aversion is higher.</p>
  </details>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title"><a href="https://research.wu.ac.at/de/publications/what-do-people-mean-by-social-mobility-dimensions-indicators-and-/">What Do People Mean by Social Mobility? Dimensions, Indicators, and the Great Gatsby Curve</a></div>
  <p class="paper-authors">with <a href="https://moritz-hoerl.github.io/">Moritz Hörl</a></p>
  <details>
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p>Rankings of social mobility depend critically on the dimension and indicator used. Societies that rank highly on one measure may rank poorly on another, yet existing research offers limited guidance for selecting among competing measures. We provide the first systematic evidence on preferences over four dimensions of social mobility (education, occupation, income, and wealth) and three indicators of income mobility: the rank–rank slope (RRS), absolute mobility (AM), and upward mobility from the bottom (CER25). Using a conjoint experiment and a within-subject rating design, we examine how a broadly representative US sample (N = 1,002) and experts (N = 99) rate different dimensions and indicators of social mobility. We embed these preference-based approaches in a social-welfare framework to shed light on the underlying assumptions of the different indicators of social mobility. We report five sets of findings. First, each indicator is an exact welfare-sufficient statistic for a distinct social objective, and no indicator satisfies all normatively appealing axioms. Second, income is the dimension the public values most, in a ranking that is stable across demographic subgroups. Third, the RRS, the indicator most prominent in the academic literature, receives the least public support. AM and CER25 receive greater support, with roughly 70% of the gap associated with lower perceived fairness. Fourth, experts share the public’s ranking of dimensions but support CER25 more and AM less. Finally, incorporating these preferences into a social mobility composite measure substantially attenuates the Great Gatsby curve and shifts US state rankings by up to 32 positions. These findings provide an empirical basis for selecting social mobility measures and designing evidence-based policy targets aligned with public preferences.</p>
  </details>
</div>

<h2 class="research-section">Ongoing Projects</h2>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">Targeting Parental Stereotypes: Investment Responses to Information about STEM</div>
  <p class="paper-authors">with <a href="https://sites.google.com/site/elisabethgsottbauer/">Elisabeth Gsottbauer</a> and <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/simonehaeckl">Simone Haeckl</a></p>
  <details>
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p>Parents' beliefs determine their investment decisions in their children's education and thereby shape children's educational trajectories. These beliefs might be biased by gender stereotypes, especially when it comes to educational and professional tracks in which women are strongly underrepresented, such as STEM fields. To investigate the influence of parental beliefs on aspirations and investment decisions, we run a preregistered information experiment with over 700 parents of daughters. We randomly assigned parents to watch a brief information video targeting either stereotypes about gender differences in innate STEM <i>ability</i> or about STEM <i>careers</i>. The control group watched a neutral video. We measure parental investment intentions as aspirations for a STEM career and through an index combining stated and revealed monetary and time allocations toward their daughter’s STEM education. We find that the Career video increases parents’ willingness to invest, particularly stated willingness to invest money, while effects on revealed outcomes are smaller. The Ability video shows little impact, and neither treatment shifts career aspirations. Results suggest that highlighting what modern STEM jobs entail is more effective than challenging beliefs about innate ability for influencing parents' investments.</p>
  </details>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">Distributing Opportunities vs Outcomes: Experimental Evidence on Wage-Setting</div>
  <details>
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p>In a dictator game with a real-effort task, participants set piece-rates either before (opportunity) or after (outcome) the work task. I test two main hypotheses. (i) Participants are more selfish when distributing outcomes rather than opportunities in a between-subjects comparison and (ii) participants become more selfish in the ex post decision in a within-subjects comparison. The hypotheses are not supported as there are no significant treatment differences between before and after decisions. The experiment is sufficiently powered and replicates established patterns in dictator games and related gender effects. These surprising null results suggest that in this specific setting, people stick with their homegrown conception of fairness and are not influenced by the timing of the piece-rate decision and therefore the lack of information on relative merit.</p>
  </details>
</div>
